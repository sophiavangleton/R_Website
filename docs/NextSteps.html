On-Campus Resources

Compiled here are the excellent resources offered on-campus!

  • Data Services at UO

Data Services assists University of Oregon students, researchers, and staff with the management, curation, and analysis of data. This is a great resource for in-person personalized help with R or other coding languages you might use.

Whats Offered:

  • Free Workshops. Data Services offers free workshops on topics like R, Python, ArcGIS, Qualtrics, and data analysis, taught by a team of faculty, staff, and peer consultants.
  • Consultations Make an appointment to get help with statistics, R, Python, GIS, Excel, surveys, version control, data analysis, or data management in-person or over Zoom.
  • Help Desk Drop by UO’s Data Services Help Desk in Knight Library for assistance with stats, R, Python, SPSS, Excel and finding and managing data. Drop-in hours are Monday to Friday 11 a.m. to 4 p.m in Knight Library. Winter GIS hours are M 1-4, T 11-3, W 1-4, and Th 11-3. Finals, breaks, and summer term are by appointment only.

The UO Library has various books and e-books on R for different uses and experience levels. Search the UO Library catalog online using, for example, R Computer program language, in the search box.

Available eBooks include:

  • Introductory Statistics with R. Peter Dalgaard; New York: Springer; 2008
  • RData Analysis without Programming. David W. Gerbing; Hoboken: Taylor and Francis; 2013
  • Applied spatial data analysis with R. Roger Bivand, Edzer J. Pebesma, Virgilio Gómez-Rubio; New York: Springer; 2008
  • Beginning R The Statistical Programming Language. Mark Gardener; Hoboken: Wiley; 2012

R is also available on all computers at the Social Science Instructional Labs (SSIL), located on the fourth floor of McKenzie Hall. You may be able to use R in other computer labs across campus. Computing labs managed by the UO Library also offers R.

Every year, the psychology department at UO hosts a R Bootcamp series for the incoming grad students covering in detail the basics of R (so basically what we are doing). The materials section is a great resource for your reference and to get more tips & tricks about cool things to do in R. A lot of what we covered is also in this series, so take a look and see if it clicks!

Limitations of R

First, let’s start with some pros. R is a great tool for statistical analysis, producing clear data visualizations and best of all is entirely free (looking at you Matlab) and open-source. R also hosts endless packages which streamline complex processes including (but not limited to) complex statistical computing, machine learning, customization data visualization, document writing (you can code up a professional looking document in R for manuscripts), website building (this website is entirely made in R), and more!

R does have some limitations however, outside of the data analysis space R is not as popular or relevant of a tool as say Python or C++. One reason why is that it is not as efficient in terms of performance, this can be a hassle when you are working with very large and complex data/tasks.

The second limitation was also mentioned in the pros, R is open-source. This means that anybody can create a package, even you! As great as this aspect is, it also means that packages vary in reliability, and some packages have not been updated or maintained so tread with caution when exploring a niche package.

Finally, you should not use R for coding up experiments like the type we usually do in our lab. For example, if you want to create high‑precision reaction‑time paradigms, R isn’t the best tool, it’s designed for analysis, not stimulus presentation. In practice, researchers often build experiments in jsPsych or PsychoPy and analyze the data in R. We use Python or Matlab with the PsychoPy addon to code most of our experiments in lab.
